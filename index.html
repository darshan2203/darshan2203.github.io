I'm a Lead Data Scientist at Target, where I lead the Retrieval team behind product search on Target.com and the Target app. For more than nine years I have worked where research meets production, turning new methods in retrieval, ranking and representation learning into large-scale production systems.

At Target, I built the company's first semantic search channel, from the embedding model to production, and led its launch as a hybrid system that now serves every search on Target.com and the app. Today I'm building LLM-based relevance evaluation for search at scale. At Rutgers, my research on fake-news detection was one of the early approaches to combine text and image signals; it has been cited 191 times and received JASIST's Top Cited Article award twice.

Selected work

Target · 2024–2025

Hybrid product search

Target's product search ran on keywords alone. I built its semantic channel from the ground up, from the embedding model and how it learns from what shoppers actually buy to the precision controls that keep results on target, and took it to production as a hybrid system alongside lexical search. It now serves every search on Target.com and the app: CTR +0.97%, conversion +0.98%, demand per visitor +1.10%, and about half as many searches that return nothing. Its embedding models are now used by Target's Ads, Content Understanding and Query Understanding teams as well.

Target · 2025–present

Automated relevance labeling and precision control

A three-stage system for judging search relevance at scale. First, an automated labeling pipeline built on agentic frameworks and state-of-the-art LLMs produces a first-generation relevance dataset with reasoning for every label. Second, that reasoning data fine-tunes an in-house LLM specialized for relevance prediction. Third, the in-house model teaches a lightweight cross-encoder or shared bi-encoder student that runs at online inference time to control precision. Each stage trades accuracy, cost and latency differently, which is why all three exist.

Target · 2025

Personalized retrieval

The same query means different things to different shoppers: "shoes" from a marathon runner and from a parent shopping for a toddler should not surface the same products. Most systems personalize late, in ranking, after retrieval has already decided what can be shown. I moved personalization into retrieval itself, with a user tower that distills a shopper's real-time, short-term and long-term behavior, a query tower and an item tower, joined by query-guided attention into a user-aware query context vector. Built as its own personalized retrieval channel, it brings in products a one-size-fits-all query vector would never reach.

Goldman Sachs · 2022–2023

Enterprise search for compliance surveillance

A search platform built for Goldman's Compliance division to find data, conduct surveillance and detect pattern anomalies across petabyte-scale firm records, later adopted firm-wide. I led the design and training of in-house retrieval models for high-recall search over sensitive, access-controlled data, where off-the-shelf models could not be used, and the multi-channel fusion of lexical and semantic results. I also helped design the sharding strategy and the entity extraction that links documents to the firm's knowledge graph.

Goldman Sachs · 2020–2022

Real-time fraud detection using a knowledge graph

Through its partnership with Apple, Goldman Sachs became the issuer of Apple Card in 2019. I worked on the program's real-time fraud detection, which screens new applications and live transactions using a knowledge graph of related entities and activity. I led feature engineering on graph-derived signals and the fraud models, and contributed to the graph's design and infrastructure.

Rutgers University · 2017–2018

Multimodal fake-news detection

Fake news rarely gives itself away in words alone; the images chosen to carry a story tell their own part of it. We taught a model to read both, grounded in how people take in and judge information, and the combined view caught more than 60% of the mistakes a text-only model made (95.18% accuracy against 86.24%). It was one of the early multimodal approaches to the problem. My part was deciding what to look for and making it work at scale, from the features to the models that bring text and images together.
